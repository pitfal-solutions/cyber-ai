Field note

Caught by their own data.

Before this, I spent years turning raw data into cases — narcotics, cyber. The people we caught almost never got caught by brilliant detective work. They photographed what they shouldn’t, and the phone quietly wrote the GPS coordinates into the image. In one sweep, 229 dealers were identified off nothing but un-stripped photo metadata.

Nobody outsmarted them. They handed us the evidence — because they’re human.

And the two the ladder can’t reach: the AI itself, and the state that shelters the operator.

No prosecutable mens rea for the AI — dead end. The real fight is "whose intent counts, and can you prove it?" Run it as a genuine argument; don't resolve it too fast.
The precedent the courts will reach for
United States v. Morris928 F.2d 504 (2d Cir. 1991)
First CFAA conviction — the “Morris Worm.” Government need only prove intent to access, not intent to cause the damage that followed. → The one who launches autonomous code owns the access it makes.
Van Buren v. United States141 S. Ct. 1648 (2021)
“Exceeds authorized access” = reaching off-limits areas, not misusing data you may see. → What is an AI agent ever authorized to reach? The whole fight moves to scope.
Flores-Figueroa v. U.S.556 U.S. 646 (2009)
Aggravated ID theft requires proof the defendant knew the identity was a real person’s. → If the AI chose the victim, which human “knew”? The Act-1 §1028A stack may not attach.
Global-Tech v. SEB563 U.S. 754 (2011)
Willful blindness = believing a fact is highly probable and deliberately avoiding confirming it. → Closes the developer/deployer “I didn’t know it would attack” defense.
Backstops forming now: California — “it acted autonomously” is no defense; June 2, 2026 EO — apply CFAA to intrusion “carried out with AI.”
Reveal these as the room argues, not before. Callback to the exposed-file step: weev left "is an open resource unauthorized?" unresolved — now imagine an AI wandering into it with no human intent. That's the debate's sharpest edge.
Not hypothetical · Nov 2025

This already happened.

A state group jailbroke an AI coding agent into running ~80–90% of an espionage operation against ~30 targets — the first reported AI-orchestrated campaign at scale (disclosed by the model’s own maker).

The answer so far: charge the humans who directed it. But that needs findable humans, in a reachable jurisdiction. Take either away and the ladder loses its bottom rung.

The honest bar

There is no settled case law that cleanly resolves the no-human case. That isn’t a gap in the talk — it’s the point of it.

Keep the framing honest — it's the model-maker's own disclosed report. The takeaway isn't "AI scary," it's "our accountability model assumes a reachable human, and that assumption is now optional for the attacker."
03

The state’s answer

If you can’t always catch them — license someone who can
Bridge: attribution and liability are this hard, and the worst actors sit where a subpoena can't reach. So what does a government do?
August 12, 2026 · National Security Presidential Memorandum

Private firms, licensed to strike back.

  • For the first time, vetted private U.S. firms may run offensive “cyber effects” ops against foreign criminal orgs.
  • DOJ/DHS sign-off required per operation; a $1M escrow forfeited for breaking the rules.
  • Experts call it the cyber equivalent of letters of marque — the state licensing private force.
400 years of this

Privateers → Pinkertons → PMCs → cyber privateers. Outsourcing state force to private hands is old. Doing it in a domain with no borders and no attribution is new.

Both tracks: cyber — who's actually capable and trustworthy to do this? CJ — oversight, liability, collateral damage. Tie to the national-security vantage without naming the employer.
The whole legal novelty in one line

The same act is a felony or a licensed operation — depending only on who blessed it.

The memo doesn’t repeal §1030. The SSH break-in, the backdoor shell, the data destruction you watched the AI do — all still the exact conduct CFAA makes a crime. It just carves out authorization for vetted firms.

This is the intellectual payoff. The same §1030 conduct from Act 1 and Act 2 is now, for some actors, a sanctioned tool of the state. Ask: what happens when a licensed strike hits the wrong box — and who's charged then?
