Part One

When a person
is behind it

The attacks costing the most today are still run by people — now with AI accelerating the work. This part looks at one real campaign end to end: what it did, how it worked, and how its operators were identified and charged.

Case study · TeamPCP (UNC6780)

A financially-motivated crew, running under a dozen names.

TeamPCP surfaced in late 2025 and quickly became one of the most active threat actors hitting the software-developer supply chain. Google's threat-intel group tracks it as UNC6780. It operated behind a rotating set of handles — PCPcat, ShellForce, DeadCatx3, Persy_PCP.

  • Motive: money — stolen credentials, cloud resources, and access resold or reused.
  • Operators charged: Ruben Thomson, 21, and Louis Gaebler, 23 — Perth, Australia.
What they did · the "Shai-Hulud" worm

A self-propagating worm through open-source packages.

The campaign (CVE-2026-45321) steals a developer's credentials, uses them to publish poisoned versions of the packages they maintain, and every build pipeline that pulls those packages becomes the next launch point. It spreads on its own — and it hit the security tools developers trust: Trivy, Bitwarden, Checkmarx, GitHub.

How they were caught · one reused image

Traced through reused accounts.

  • Started from one alias — DeadCatx3.
  • A GitHub handle led to a bug-bounty profile with a real name.
  • That profile listed the campaign's own command-server domain.
  • A reused password opened stolen-credential databases — and personal Google, TikTok, and Steam accounts.
  • A reused cat-at-monitors avatar linked the Steam profile to TeamPCP's Telegram.
  • Two operators arrested in Perth, Australia.

Attribution came from operator mistakes — reused handles, passwords, and an avatar — not from breaking the malware.

From discussion to a working method

How each side would actually handle it.

Defender / analyst CYBER
  • Map each step to ATT&CK; confirm with independent detection, not the attacker's claims.
  • Contain at the earliest reliable signal — revoke the session, block the source.
  • Fix the root cause — the broken access control and the guessable reset — not just the symptom.
  • Preserve the proxy and detector logs as the evidence record.
Investigator / prosecutor JUSTICE
  • Build on artifacts, not narrative — payloads and status codes show intent and success.
  • Lead with the strongest count — the completed takeover / identity theft.
  • Charge the contested step in the alternative; don't stake the case on it.
  • Track the victim-side duty — breach notification — separately.

Discussion — tactics CYBER
  • With no human to fingerprint, what's left to attribute on?Behavior and infrastructure — command-and-control, tooling, timing and velocity — not an identity. Attribution shifts from who to what.
  • What did the agent do that a fixed script wouldn't — how do you spot adaptation?It re-planned when blocked and chained new techniques on the fly. You catch that with behavioral / anomaly detection, not signatures.

Discussion — accountability JUSTICE
  • No one chose the path — so whose intent do you prove?You have to find a human's intent somewhere in the chain — the AI's has none the law can charge.
  • Developer, deployer, operator, the user who typed the goal — who do you charge?Whoever's intent or willful blindness you can prove — usually the operator or user who set the goal; the developer only if they ignored a known risk.

Part Three

The current
predicament

The tools moved first. Attribution and the law were built for a slower world, and the gap is now wide open. Here is what that looks like in practice — and how the courts and defenders are scrambling to close it, in real time, over just the last year.

The legal gap

You can't charge the AI. So you climb a chain of humans.

The AI has no criminal intent to prosecute — a dead end. Liability has to attach to a person behind it, and prosecutors are converging on four tests — intent, foreseeability, control, safeguards — none of which cleanly points at one name.

End-user

Typed the goal

Intended this — or just "test my network"?

Operator

Ran / supervised it

What controls were set? Was anyone watching?

Deployer

Gave it tools

Handed the model network access and a shell.

Developer

Built the model

Set — or failed to set — the limits.

And two the chain often can't reach: the AI itself, and the state sheltering the operator.

The law · the last twelve months

Decades-old statutes, stretched over brand-new facts.

  • Colorado tried to lead — then hit reverse. Its first-in-the-nation AI Act was postponed, had enforcement stayed, then was repealed and rewritten inside a year. The rules aren't settled; they're being drafted live.
  • Prosecutors reach for old tools. The CFAA (1986) and cases like Van Buren (2021) were never written for an attacker with no human intent — a June 2026 order simply directs that they apply to AI intrusion anyway.
  • Still no settled answer. No court has squarely resolved who is liable when no human chose the attack. It's the open question — not a solved one.
  • The pressure is real. The FBI logged roughly $893M in AI-enabled fraud losses across 22,000+ complaints in 2025 — the scale forcing all of this.
The defense · what's actually deployed today

You can't fingerprint the attacker — so you watch its behavior.

Detect BEHAVIOR, NOT IDENTITY
  • AI-native behavioral detection — flag an agent acting out of character: an inventory bot that suddenly runs DROP TABLE or reads sensitive directories.
  • Map to MITRE ATLAS — the ATT&CK for AI systems (80+ techniques) that detection teams now build logic against.
  • Continuous automated red-teaming — assume the agent is hostile, and keep testing it.
Contain AT MACHINE SPEED
  • Runtime, context-aware containment — halt one bad action without killing the whole workflow.
  • Zero-trust microsegmentation — scope every agent's credentials, network, and tools tightly.
  • Provider-side takedowns — model makers cutting off misuse themselves (Anthropic disrupting GTG-1002; OpenAI catching its own model mid-breach).

The through-line: with no human to catch, defense shifts from who did it to what is this doing, right now.

The policy answer · August 2026

If you can't catch them, license someone who can.

When attribution and reach fail, one response is to deputize the private sector. A 2026 national-security memorandum lets vetted private firms run offensive operations against foreign criminal networks — per-operation government sign-off, money held in escrow against misuse. Observers call it the cyber equivalent of letters of marque.

The legal novelty, in one line

The same act is a felony or a licensed operation — depending only on who authorized it.

The memo doesn't repeal the computer-crime laws from Part I. The intrusion, the shell, the data destruction the agent performed are still exactly the conduct §1030 makes a crime. It only carves out authorization for vetted firms. Identical technique; opposite legal status.
