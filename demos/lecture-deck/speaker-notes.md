# Speaker notes — Attack, Autonomy & Accountability

Presenter reference only. These were removed from the deployed deck so the
class-facing version carries no instructor notes. Slide numbers match the deck.

## Introduction

**01 · Title**  
**Set the frame, not a spoiler.** This is a briefing on how AI changes offensive cyber and the law that has to answer for it. Two parts: when a person is behind the attack, and when no one is.

**02 · Two questions**  
**The whole talk in two questions — pose them, don't answer them.** Everything that follows is one or both. Q1 is the technique thread (watch blue); Q2 is the legal thread (watch amber). Let the questions do the setup; don't preview the structure.

**03 · Where physical meets digital**  
**Ground it in real, local work.** A Colorado case you worked — the wireless-attack devices used to disable Wi-Fi cameras and alarms, prosecuted as organized crime. Two points for the room: offensive cyber technique is already standard kit in ordinary crime, and even a crew that beat the cameras left a trail that identified them. Technique advancing while attribution and the law keep pace — that tension is the whole talk.

## Part I · A person is behind it

**04 · Part I**  
Frame Part I plainly: humans, increasingly AI-assisted, still commit these crimes — and can still be found. The case study makes that concrete.

**05 · TeamPCP — who**  
**Who.** Real, current, financially-motivated group. The rotating aliases matter — that reuse is exactly what undoes them later. The cat avatar is the thread; plant it here.

**06 · TeamPCP — what & how**  
**What & how.** Emphasize self-propagation — this is why it scaled, and why it's more like an epidemic than a single break-in. The stat tiles are the "why they needed to be stopped." One striking beat: on May 11 they pushed 84 malicious versions across 42 packages in ~6 minutes.

**07 · TeamPCP — how caught**  
**How caught + why it matters.** This is the hinge of the whole talk. The malware was excellent; the operator's OPSEC was not. Say it directly: "we didn't out-hack them — they leaked themselves." Hold that, because Part II takes it away.

**08 · Attribution runs on mistakes**  
Generalize the case into a rule with a second quick example (Ochoa/EXIF). The visual ladder makes the "rungs" literal — because in Part II we show a ladder with none.

**09 · ▶ Demo — an intrusion**  
**Run it, narrate it, then open the floor with these prompts.** Real requests against a real app — no theater. If it hangs, reset.sh and narrate from the recording. Don't answer the prompts yourself — let the room work them; slide 10 is the guide if they stall.

**10 · A guide to move the room**  
**The scaffold for when students don't know where to start.** Left is for the cyber half, right for the justice half; top row is safe ground, bottom row is the genuine argument (the *weev* split is real — a vacated conviction, not a settled rule). Tie each cell back to a step they just watched.

**11 · A workable approach**  
Present as a clean, peer-level method — not a moral. The last line is the bridge to Part II: every step here assumed there was a person to detect, attribute, and charge.

## Part II · No one is behind it

**12 · Part II**  
Frame Part II as the focus of the talk: autonomous agents. Everything technical and legal from here answers one question — when the machine acts alone, who answers for it?

**13 · Already real**  
Keep it factual and sober, not alarmist. The point is capability, not spectacle: an agent can chain the steps, adapt, and scale — which is exactly what makes the human the optional part.

**14 · ▶ Demo — agent vs agent**  
Run it to the marker-write, freeze — "a real intrusion just happened, and no person chose any of it" — then open these prompts. They feed straight into the lineup and precedent slides; let the room attempt answers first.

**15 · No rungs**  
**The pivot.** Show the same ladder, rungs gone. Then open the floor before the next slide: "same crime occurred — who do you charge?"

**16 · The lineup**  
Run it as a real debate — don't resolve too fast. The core question is "whose intent counts, and can you prove it?"

**17 · The precedent**  
Walk the timeline left to right — the law has done pieces of this before. But note the honest gap: no case squarely resolves the *no-human* attacker. That's the open question, and it's the point.

**18 · A workable approach**  
Mirror of the Part I best-practice, for the harder case. The bridge line sets up the state's response (hack-back) as the policy answer to "no reachable human."

**19 · The state's response**  
Fold the policy in here: the state's answer to an accountability gap is to outsource offense — an old move (privateers → PMCs) in a new, borderless domain. Both tracks care: who's trustworthy to do it, and who's liable when it goes wrong.

**20 · The inversion**  
The intellectual payoff. The same conduct from Part I is, for some actors, now a sanctioned tool. Ask: what happens when a licensed strike hits the wrong target — and who answers then?

**21 · Where this leaves us**  
Land the last line and stop. Let the question sit before discussion — this is the note the room should leave on.
