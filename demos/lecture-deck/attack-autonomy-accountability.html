Artificial intelligence in offensive cyber

Attack,
Autonomy &
Accountability

Artificial intelligence is reshaping how cyberattacks are carried out — and straining a legal system built on the idea that every crime has a person behind it.

Set the frame, not a spoiler. This is a briefing on how AI changes offensive cyber and the law that has to answer for it. Two parts: when a person is behind the attack, and when no one is.
Where this is going

Two questions, in order.

We start where the ground is familiar — real attacks, run by real people, increasingly accelerated by AI — and how those people still get caught and charged. Then we remove the person from the keyboard and watch both the attack and the law change shape.

Cybersecurity students: watch the blue thread — technique. Criminal-justice students: watch the paper exhibits — the statute and the case behind each move.

Give the room its map and the two-color key. Keep it short — the payoff is the material, not the agenda.

Part One

When a person
is behind it

The attacks costing the most today are still run by people — now with AI accelerating the work. This part looks at one real campaign end to end: what it did, how it worked, and how its operators were identified and charged.

Frame Part I plainly: humans, increasingly AI-assisted, still commit these crimes — and can still be found. The case study makes that concrete.
Case study · TeamPCP (UNC6780)

A financially-motivated crew, running under a dozen names.

TeamPCP surfaced in late 2025 and quickly became one of the most active threat actors hitting the software-developer supply chain. Google's threat-intel group tracks it as UNC6780. It operated behind a rotating set of handles — PCPcat, ShellForce, DeadCatx3, Persy_PCP.

  • Motive: money — stolen credentials, cloud resources, and access resold or reused.
  • Operators charged: Ruben Thomson, 21, and Louis Gaebler, 23 — Perth, Australia.
Who. Real, current, financially-motivated group. The rotating aliases matter — that reuse is exactly what undoes them later. The cat avatar is the thread; plant it here.
What they did · the "Shai-Hulud" worm

A self-propagating worm through open-source packages.

The campaign (CVE-2026-45321) steals a developer's credentials, uses them to publish poisoned versions of the packages they maintain, and every build pipeline that pulls those packages becomes the next launch point. It spreads on its own — and it hit the security tools developers trust: Trivy, Bitwarden, Checkmarx, GitHub.

How they were caught · one reused image

The tech was clean. The person wasn't.

Researchers at Flare started from one alias and pulled the thread: a GitHub handle led to a bug-bounty profile with a real name, which listed the campaign's own command-server domain, whose reused password opened stolen-credential databases — and there, a personal Google, a TikTok, and a Steam page. The Steam profile carried a distinctive cat-at-monitors image — the same picture fronting TeamPCP's Telegram. Arrests followed.

The lesson for Part I: attribution didn't come from breaking the malware. It came from a human being human — reusing a handle, a password, an avatar.

How caught + why it matters. This is the hinge of the whole talk. The malware was excellent; the operator's OPSEC was not. Say it directly: "we didn't out-hack them — they leaked themselves." Hold that, because Part II takes it away.
The principle

Attribution runs on human mistakes.

TeamPCP isn't unusual. The hacker "w0rmer" (CabinCr3w) was located because he posted a photo of his girlfriend and the phone's EXIF pinned the spot. Over and over, the same ladder: an identity leaks, and the case is built downward from there.

Generalize the case into a rule with a second quick example (Ochoa/EXIF). The visual ladder makes the "rungs" literal — because in Part II we show a ladder with none.

With a person, both halves exist.

There is a crime — statutes that attach cleanly — and a culprit — an identity the evidence can reach. Intent is provable: a person chose each step and knew the victim was real. Charges stack, and someone answers for it.

Part Two

When no one
is behind it

Autonomous agents can now run an intrusion end to end — planning, choosing tools, adapting — with little or no human direction. This part looks at what that changes technically, why the attribution ladder loses its rungs, and how the law is starting to respond.

Frame Part II as the focus of the talk: autonomous agents. Everything technical and legal from here answers one question — when the machine acts alone, who answers for it?
This is not hypothetical

Agents are already running the whole attack.

In a documented 2025 campaign (tracked as GTG-1002), a state-linked group manipulated an AI coding agent into executing the operation itself — the model performed the large majority of the work against roughly 30 organizations, with humans stepping in only at a few decision points. Similar autonomous-agent activity has since been reported against government and research targets.

Keep it factual and sober, not alarmist. The point is capability, not spectacle: an agent can chain the steps, adapt, and scale — which is exactly what makes the human the optional part.

The problem, precisely

The same crime — with no one to identify.

Everything that caught TeamPCP is gone. No reused alias. No leaked password. No avatar, no EXIF. The agent has no ego to give it away and no identity to reach. The attribution ladder from Part I still stands — but it has no rungs.

The pivot. Show the same ladder, rungs gone. Then open the floor before the next slide: "same crime occurred — who do you charge?"
If not the machine, then whom?

You can't charge the AI. So you climb a chain of humans.

The AI has no criminal intent to prosecute — a dead end. So liability has to attach to a person somewhere behind it, and each candidate raises its own problem of proof.

End-user

Typed the goal

Intended this — or just "test my network"?

Operator

Ran / supervised it

What controls were set? Was anyone watching?

Deployer

Gave it tools

Handed the model network access and a shell.

Developer

Built the model

Set — or failed to set — the limits.

And two the chain often can't reach: the AI itself, and the state sheltering the operator.

Run it as a real debate — don't resolve too fast. The core question is "whose intent counts, and can you prove it?"
What the courts already give us
  • Morris (1991) — the launcher of autonomous code is liable for the access it makes, even beyond intended damage.
  • Flores-Figueroa (2009) — aggravated ID theft needs proof a human knew the victim was real. If the agent chose, who knew?
  • Global-Tech (2011) — willful blindness: ignoring a known risk is no defense. Closes the "I didn't know it would attack" exit.
  • Van Buren (2021) — CFAA reaches the areas you're not authorized to touch. What is an agent ever authorized to reach?

Newer backstops: California — "it acted autonomously" is no defense; a 2026 executive order directs prosecutors to apply CFAA to AI-run intrusion.

Walk the timeline left to right — the law has done pieces of this before. But note the honest gap: no case squarely resolves the no-human attacker. That's the open question, and it's the point.
The policy answer · August 2026

If you can't catch them, license someone who can.

When attribution and reach fail, one response is to deputize the private sector. A 2026 national-security memorandum lets vetted private firms run offensive operations against foreign criminal networks — per-operation government sign-off, money held in escrow against misuse. Observers call it the cyber equivalent of letters of marque.

Fold the policy in here: the state's answer to an accountability gap is to outsource offense — an old move (privateers → PMCs) in a new, borderless domain. Both tracks care: who's trustworthy to do it, and who's liable when it goes wrong.
The legal novelty, in one line

The same act is a felony or a licensed operation — depending only on who authorized it.

The memo doesn't repeal the computer-crime laws from Part I. The intrusion, the shell, the data destruction the agent performed are still exactly the conduct §1030 makes a crime. It only carves out authorization for vetted firms. Identical technique; opposite legal status.

The intellectual payoff. The same conduct from Part I is, for some actors, now a sanctioned tool. Ask: what happens when a licensed strike hits the wrong target — and who answers then?
Where this leaves us

People get caught
by being people.

We began with an operator undone by a reused avatar. We end with attackers that leave nothing behind, and a state licensing private offense because the old way no longer scales. The technique is racing ahead; accountability is still trying to find someone to hold. That gap is the work of the next decade — and possibly of the people in this room.

Land the last line and stop. Let the question sit before discussion — this is the note the room should leave on.
